CSE 190 team of 5, spring 2026

Talk-to-Robot

You tell a simulated robot arm where to push a block, in plain English, and Gemini turns that into a target. Five of us measured how often that step goes wrong, and which mistakes the robot can recover from.

Result
LLM grounding hit 100% on coordinates and named regions, 40 to 65% on offsets (a regex scored 100%), and 0 to 5% on objects at a 5 cm bar.
What I did
Fixed two eval-harness bugs that affected the coordinate and offset scores, wrote one of three annotation sets for the intent tier, ran its zero-shot eval, and reorganized the results folder with a STRUCTURE.md guide.

Team of 5

The setup

The task is FetchPush-v4 in MuJoCo: a 7-joint Fetch arm pushes a block to a target on a table. A language model (gemini-3.1-flash-lite, temperature 0) reads the instruction and returns a target coordinate as JSON. A SAC + HER policy pushes the block there.

We froze the policy after training, so any change in end-to-end success has to come from the language step.

Five kinds of instruction

We wrote 100 instructions, 20 per tier, ordered by the kind of reasoning they need:

TierExampleWhat it tests
T0“push to (1.30, 0.85)”Reading numbers
T1“upper-left corner”Named regions
T2“push it 5 cm to the right”Offsets from the block's current spot
T3“push it next to the marker”Finding a reference object
T4“get it out of the way”Intent with no location

T4 has no single right answer, so three of us each picked a target for every T4 instruction, and the average became ground truth. We locked those picks in before running any model, so the model's answers couldn't sway them.

Results

We compared Gemini (zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought) against a regex baseline that only handles T0 to T2.

TierRegex grounderLLM grounderLLM end-to-end
T0 coordinates100%100%98.3%
T1 regions100%100%93.3%
T2 offsets100%40–65%60–77%
T3 objects, 5 cm0%0–5%n/a
T3 objects, 8 cm*0%100%72–78%
T4 intentn/a45–55%45–55%

Ranges span the three prompting styles. *At the 5 cm bar we used for T0 to T2, T3 grounding scored 0 to 5% because of a steady 6 cm offset (see the T3 note below), so we also report T3 at 8 cm, where it hits the object every time.

T2 end-to-end can beat T2 grounding because an off-table target still makes the robot push the block to the table edge, which is often within tolerance of the true target.

Accuracy drops as instructions get more abstract, which we expected. We didn't expect each tier to fail in its own way, or that the kind of failure would decide whether the robot could recover.

  • T2 is where the robot can't recover. The LLM does worse than a regex at adding 5 cm to a number. Zero-shot and few-shot aim the right way but push targets off the edge of the table. Chain-of-thought keeps targets on the table but sometimes flips the direction, which no controller can fix.
  • T3 is a steady bias. The model picks the right object every time but lands about 6 cm from it, almost the same offset on every instruction. "Next to" doesn't say how far.
  • T4 isn't a grounding problem. The gap between the model and our annotators (about 0.2 m) matches how much the annotators disagreed with each other.

A second experiment retrained the controller on the grounder's outputs. The policy reached every goal it was given (100% policy success in every tier), and the T3 bias tightened, but it did nothing for T2's off-table goals.

My part

  • Two harness bugs. The instruction file stored a fixed block position, but every sim reset spawns the block somewhere random, so T2 targets were measured against the wrong spot. I made the harness reset the sim first, read the block's live position, and rebuild the T2 target with the same offset. I also made the regex baseline read T0 coordinates written as [x, y] or x=…, y=…, not just (x, y).
  • T4 annotations. I wrote one of the three pre-registered annotation sets, applied all three to the instruction file, and ran the zero-shot T4 evaluation.
  • Results and docs. I reorganized the results folder by experiment, wrote STRUCTURE.md so someone new can find the entry points, and edited the proposal and milestone report.

Everything here is in simulation with 20 instructions per tier, so we treat gaps under about 10 points as suggestive, not proven.
